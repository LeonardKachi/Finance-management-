Investing 101: where the money can go

Every option here trades risk for potential return differently. None of them are wrong - the right mix depends on when you'll need the money and how much volatility you can sit through without panicking.

High-yield savings accounts Low risk
Typical return: around 4-4.5% APY in a normal rate environment, versus roughly 0.4% for a standard savings account

A savings account with a bank that pays meaningfully more interest than the standard rate, usually offered by online-only banks with lower overhead. Your money stays fully accessible.

Why it's good: it's where your emergency fund and near-term goals belong - no risk of loss, and the interest at least partly offsets inflation.

Watch out for: rates move with central bank policy, so today's rate isn't locked in. It won't build long-term wealth on its own.

Certificates of deposit / fixed deposits Low risk
Typical return: similar to or slightly above high-yield savings, fixed for the term

You lock money away for a set term - three months to several years - in exchange for a guaranteed rate that won't fall even if market rates do.

Why it's good: predictable, useful for money you know you won't need before a specific date.

Watch out for: early withdrawal usually costs you a penalty, and your money is locked out of better opportunities if rates rise later.

Government and corporate bonds Low to medium risk
Typical return: modest, historically a few points above cash, moving with interest rates

You're lending money to a government or a company for a fixed period in exchange for regular interest payments and your principal back at the end. Government bonds from stable countries carry the least risk; corporate bonds pay a bit more for taking on the company's credit risk.

Why it's good: steadier than stocks, and bond prices often hold up or rise when stock markets fall, which is why they're used to smooth out a portfolio.

Watch out for: bond prices fall when interest rates rise, and corporate bonds carry the risk that the company defaults.

Individual stocks (shares) High risk, per company
Typical return: highly variable - a single company can lose most of its value or multiply many times over

A share is a unit of ownership in one specific company. Buy a share of a company and you own a small slice of its profits, its losses, and its future.

Why it's good: if you pick well and hold long enough, the upside is uncapped, and you share directly in a company's growth.

Watch out for: concentration risk. A handful of individual stocks can swing your entire portfolio on one earnings report, one lawsuit, one competitor. Most beginners are better served starting with funds, below, and treating individual stocks as a smaller, separate bet if they choose to make one at all.

Index funds and ETFs Medium risk, diversified
Typical return: the S&P 500 has averaged roughly 10% a year nominally since 1926, around 7% after inflation, though any single year can swing sharply in either direction

A single fund that holds hundreds or thousands of companies at once, tracking a market index like the S&P 500 rather than trying to beat it. An ETF trades like a stock during the day; an index mutual fund prices once a day - the underlying holdings are usually near-identical.

Why it's good: instant diversification, very low fees (often well under 0.2% a year), and no need to pick winners - you own the whole market's average outcome, which historically beats most professional stock-pickers over time.

Watch out for: it moves with the whole market, so a bad year for stocks is a bad year for your index fund too. It rewards patience over years, not months.

Actively managed mutual funds Medium risk, diversified
Typical return: varies by manager; most underperform a comparable index fund after fees over long periods

Similar to an index fund - a pooled basket of investments - but run by a manager actively picking what to buy and sell, trying to beat the market rather than match it.

Why it's good: professional oversight and, occasionally, genuine skill in specific niches or market conditions.

Watch out for: higher fees, often 1% a year or more, which compound against you over decades. The evidence consistently shows most active funds don't beat their benchmark index by enough to justify the extra cost.

Real estate and REITs Medium risk
Typical return: historically comparable to stocks over the very long run, with different timing of ups and downs

Owning property directly, or buying a Real Estate Investment Trust - a fund that owns income-producing property and pays out most of its rental income as dividends, tradable like a stock.

Why it's good: a REIT gives real estate exposure without needing a deposit, a mortgage, or a tenant to manage, and adds a different kind of diversification to a stock-and-bond portfolio.

Watch out for: direct property is illiquid and capital-intensive; REITs are sensitive to interest rates and the property sector they focus on.

Retirement and pension accounts Depends what's inside
Typical return: whatever the underlying investments earn - the account itself is a tax wrapper, not an asset

A tax-advantaged account for long-term retirement savings - the exact name and rules depend on the country: 401(k) and IRA in the US, pension schemes in the UK and EU, and similar structures elsewhere. Money inside is usually invested in funds you choose.

Why it's good: tax relief on contributions or withdrawals, and often an employer match - free money for showing up. Starting in your 20s rather than your 30s can nearly double the final balance for the same monthly contribution, purely from extra compounding time.

Watch out for: money is typically locked away until a set retirement age, with penalties for early withdrawal, so it isn't the place for funds you might need sooner.

Cryptocurrency Very high risk
Typical return: extremely wide historical range in both directions - not comparable to the asset classes above

Digital assets like Bitcoin or Ethereum, traded on crypto exchanges rather than regulated stock markets in most jurisdictions.

Why it's good: for those who understand the technology and can tolerate large swings, it offers exposure to a genuinely new asset class not correlated with traditional markets in the same way.

Watch out for: extreme volatility, weaker regulatory protection than stocks or bonds, and a real risk of losing most or all of what you put in. Treat it, if at all, as a small slice of money you could afford to lose entirely - never the emergency fund or the rent.

How the stock market actually works

The mechanics behind the numbers above, in plain terms.

~7%

~74%
Of the last 100 years the market finished up rather than down
0
Reliable way to know which specific days those gains will land on

A share is ownership, not a lottery ticket. When you buy a share, you own a tiny slice of a real company - its factories, its brands, its future profits. The price moves because buyers and sellers keep re-guessing what that slice of future profit is worth, based on earnings, interest rates, competitors, and plain sentiment.

You get paid two ways. The share price can rise over time as the company grows - that's a capital gain, only real once you sell. Some companies also pay out part of their profit directly to shareholders on a regular schedule - that's a dividend, paid whether or not you sell.

Volatility is the price of admission, not a warning sign. Markets fall sharply and often - double-digit drops happen most decades. What the long-run averages above hide is that they're built from wildly uneven years: strong gains most of the time, sharp losses some of the time, and no way to know in advance which is coming. Selling during a drop locks in the loss; staying invested is what lets the long-run average apply to you.

Diversification is the free lunch. Owning one company means one bad decision by one CEO can wipe out your position. Owning an index fund of hundreds of companies means no single failure sinks the whole investment - some companies will do badly in any given year, but the broad economy tends to grow.

Dollar-cost averaging turns volatility into a non-event. Investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule - say, monthly - means you automatically buy more units when prices are low and fewer when prices are high, without ever having to predict which is which. It's less about timing the market and more about removing the need to.

Time is the one input you can't buy back. Two people investing the same monthly amount at the same return, ten years apart, will not end up with similar totals - the earlier starter usually ends up with roughly double, sometimes more, purely because their money compounded for longer. That's the entire argument for starting now with a small amount rather than waiting for a larger one later.

What each rule means

None of these are laws. They're starting points, built for a typical single income with no unusual circumstances.

Net x 240
Same math as the 4% safe withdrawal rate, just expressed monthly. 240 months is 20 years, and withdrawing 5% a year from that portfolio replaces one year of income indefinitely under typical historical market returns.
Gross x 0.20 (invest)
A savings rate high enough to reach the 240x target in roughly 25 to 30 years for most income and return combinations - sooner if returns run hot or you increase the rate over time.
Net x 0.60 / Gross x 0.30
Two different housing rules on purpose. 30% of gross for rent alone is the traditional affordability line; 60% of net is the outer limit once utilities, insurance, and upkeep are folded in.
Net x 0.20 (spend)
The relief valve in the 50/30/20 budget - wants and discretionary spending, guilt-free because it's already budgeted for.
Expenses x 6
Six months of actual spending, not income - covers a job loss or income gap without touching investments. Some tighten this to 3 months with very stable income, or extend it to 9 to 12 with variable income.
Gross x 0.36 (total debt)
The back-end ratio mortgage lenders use: all debt payments, including housing, under 36% of gross income. Above 43% is where most conventional loans stop qualifying.
Gross x 0.10 (car)
Often paired with a 20% down payment and a loan term capped at 4 years - buy less car than you can technically finance.
Annual gross x 10 (life insurance)
A rough starting point for term life coverage if others depend on your income. Add outstanding debt and future costs like education on top; subtract existing savings and coverage.
Rule of 72
Divide 72 by your expected annual return to estimate how many years it takes an investment to double. At 7%, that's roughly 10 years; at 4%, roughly 18.
Retirement benchmark
A widely cited target: roughly 1x your annual salary saved by 30, 3x by 40, 6x by 50, 8x by 60, and 10x by 67. It assumes continuous saving and market-rate returns, so treat it as a checkpoint, not a verdict.
Worth knowing before you act on any of this: these are general guidelines, not financial advice, and none of them account for your tax situation, dependents, existing debt, or local cost of living. The 20% investing rule and the 20% guilt-free rule use the same percentage on different bases (gross vs. net), so don't assume they're interchangeable. Housing rules overlap by design - check both, and let the stricter one win. If you're carrying high-interest debt, paying that down usually outperforms the investing target above.
